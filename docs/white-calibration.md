# Measured white-window prototype

Run `build/hdr-probe.exe --white-calibrate` in the build directory to locate the center patch by a dark/bright difference and start a sweep. Alternatively select the center ROI manually and press T. The camera must be pointed at the stationary screen. V replays the valid corrected curve, U selects uncorrected white, and Escape exits. Repeated runs write timestamped observation and curve CSVs.

The center probe occupies a fixed 1% rectangle. Surround area grows from 1% to 100%. For this first experiment both the white surround and center receive the same trial correction, so the result is a uniformly signaled white window. The shader has separate probe and surround signal parameters, allowing later independent surround experiments. Calibration reads only the center ROI, never the whole camera image.

The reference is the camera reading of a 1% white patch requested at 100 signal nits. Exposure is adjusted before that sweep to bring the camera reading into a usable region, then frozen. White balance and gain stay fixed. No camera ratio is interpreted as a luminance ratio, and 100 signal nits is not a measurement of panel output.

Before each sweep, use a binary 20/100-signal brightness pattern at the center to estimate the delay from presentation to received camera observations. Fit a delayed two-level response in camera code space; the fit does not interpret those codes as light. Save/log the measured delay and reject a pattern with insufficient contrast or an unbounded delay. This is an effective display-to-camera delay, including panel response, exposure integration, transport, and scheduling; it does not isolate sensor latency.

The same pattern now measures observed transition onset and stabilization: compare each changed edge with its final three-frame plateau and identify the first sample after which the remaining observations stay in a noise-aware camera-code band. Use the 90th percentile across resolved edges as the stabilization estimate. Set post-delay settling to max(50 ms, observed stabilization minus effective delay). Save timing details in `white-result-<id>.json.timing.json`, including median received frame interval and estimated time for a three-frame measurement. Synthetic tests cover a pure delayed step and a delayed slow response. If the pattern cannot establish at least four stable transitions, calibration stops rather than assuming 50 ms is sufficient.

These measurements characterize the observed camera/display chain for the tested brightness steps, not a photodiode gray-to-gray specification or a guarantee for all load transitions. The constant one-percent probe pattern does not fully characterize load-dependent ABL time constants; such transitions may require their own timing tests.

At each area, wait for the measured delay plus 50 ms settling, then average exactly three fresh camera frames. Poll at up to 100 Hz; the actual sampling rate is limited by camera delivery and display refresh. Sample callbacks record frame sequence, sample timestamp, and host arrival. Reused frames are excluded; independence and exact sensor exposure timing are not established by this alone. The previous six-frame/one-second measurement scheme is superseded.

Exposure starts at -8 or shorter and is adjusted before the sweep to target a lower camera-code region (mean approximately 25..90, avoiding high channel values). Exposure stays fixed after timing measurement. This is an empirically chosen observation range, not a claim that every webcam has greater physical sensitivity there.

Compare the observed center camera code to the reference within a noise-aware tolerance. Bracket the required signal using fixed multiplicative steps, then bisect geometrically. Those signal-space steps are search choices, not inferred brightness multipliers. Increasing the signal also increases panel load because the entire window is corrected; each candidate is physically displayed and remeasured.

Initial area anchors are 1%, 10%, 20%, 40%, 60%, 80%, 100%. Interpolate correction in log-signal space. Verify interval midpoints through new measurements and solve/refine only failed predictions. Additional checks subdivide failed intervals down to roughly four percentage points, with a 24-knot refinement limit and 540-second run deadline. Coarse successful intervals retain few knots. Final low-load reference checks detect drift. A valid result is conditional on these sampled checks, not a proof of all-area or all-scene uniformity.

After coarse fitting, fine calibration samples the complete area range at two-percentage-point increments (3%, 5%, ... 99%, then 100%; 1% is the reference). Every point starts from the current interpolated correction. If it misses the camera target, solve it again and add nearby points at one percentage point, then half a percentage point if needed. Retain the measured fine points for interpolation. The same exposure, measured camera delay, 50 ms settling, and three-frame averages apply. This typically adds tens of seconds rather than minutes, depending on how many points need correction.

Before accepting the fine curve, check ten unused/repeated areas in descending order without changing the fitted correction. A failed reverse check rejects the curve and flags possible drift or panel dynamics; additional sampling alone does not necessarily resolve these effects. The final low-load reference must also pass. Fine refinement is bounded at 160 knots and the existing run deadline. The target tolerance is in camera codes and is noise-aware; a perceptual uniformity guarantee cannot be inferred directly from that tolerance.

The solver rejects clipping, invalid exposure, nonconvergence, unreachable targets, and reference drift. It does not automatically lower the target and silently declare success. Cancelled/failed runs keep observation files and do not enable corrected replay. Three-frame averages no longer provide the previous extended stability test. The search assumes a locally monotone camera response versus trial signal; panel plateaus or complex tone mapping can violate that assumption.

On success, automatically alternate eight-second smooth expansions from 1% to 100%: first uncorrected, then corrected, repeating. The preview title identifies the current pass. Both passes use the same area trajectory and reset to the small window between passes. The animation updates with rendering rather than being limited to camera sampling. V restarts the alternating comparison; U selects manual uncorrected mode.

The companion window includes an eight-second rolling graph of the ROI mean camera code, smoothed by a three-frame moving average. Orange denotes the uncalibrated replay, green the calibrated replay, and the dotted line the measured reference. The vertical axis is labeled in camera codes and automatically scales to the visible range. Replay colors account for the estimated camera delay. The graph clears when exposure or ROI changes.

Automatic ROI selection now grows a broad interior rectangle in the detected center patch, averaging all its pixels. Small regions without at least a 17-by-17-pixel interior are rejected. A manual ROI may cover the entire sensor if it views only the fixed screen probe; including changing surround pixels would contaminate the center-response measurement.

Camera flare from the changing surround can contaminate the center reading. This first prototype has not separated flare, unknown camera processing, and physical panel response. Shielding/framing checks and independent optical validation remain important before relying on the result. Report uniformity in camera code differences rather than physical percentage brightness error.

The observation matcher includes a synthetic test across nonlinear camera transfers and an unreachable target: `build/hdr-probe.exe --self-test`. No desktop profiles, hooks, or monitor settings are modified by this calibration; correction is applied only inside the generator.
